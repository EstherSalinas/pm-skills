## 1. The North Star statement exercise, in full

Do this qualitatively, top-down, **before** naming any actual metric. It's tempting to rush
to numbers — resist that; teams that skip this and go straight to metric-naming tend to
disagree later about what the metric even means, because they never agreed on the underlying
idea. "We've done entire workshops just chipping away at this statement," notes co-author
John Cutler — that's not wasted time, it's the part that makes the metric mean something.

Four levels, each filling in the blank for the level above:

**North Star** — *"Our path to medium-to-long-term sustainable (and defensible or
differentiated) growth is a function of our ability to: ___."*
Qualities to aim for: ideally customer-centric, less directly addressable, stable,
mid/long-term oriented, specific and descriptive, **not revenue**.

**Inputs** — *"...which is a function of our ability to: [X, Y, and Z]."*
Qualities: more addressable, "part of the formula," a variable/puzzle piece, not work
itself, persistent (lasts quarters/years).

**Opportunities** — *"...we believe the key opportunities and leverage points to influence
these inputs are: ___."*
Qualities: a leverage point, inspires multiple options, lasts months/quarters,
solution-agnostic but directional/opinionated.

**Interventions** — *"...and some representative options to make tangible progress
include: ___."*
Qualities: more work-like, more experiment-like, will change, time-bound.

Don't treat this as boxes to fill in mechanically — encourage introspection and fine-tune the
wording as a team (or, solo, sit with it rather than taking the first phrasing). If someone
can't explain the North Star qualitatively, they won't be able to explain it quantitatively
either — that's the whole reason this step exists before the metric-naming step.

## 2. Surfacing beliefs (templates)

Every person approaches a business problem with beliefs and biases — assumptions about what
customers value, theories about market direction, opinions about the product's differentiated
value. Failing to surface these leaves unresolved tension and blind spots. Use these
fill-in-the-blank prompts (there are no wrong answers — the goal is casting a wide net):

- *"Our direct competition is [ ]. We are disrupting legacy options like [ ]. In turn,
  [ ] may disrupt us."*
- *"One technology change that will affect us is [ ]. This change will impact us because
  [ ]. As a response, we will [ ]."*
- *"I think the real reason we win new customers is because [ ]."*
- *"I believe [some behavior in our market] is due to [reason]."*
- *"If we focus on meeting [a specific customer need] and do that extremely well, we will
  have some leeway when it comes to [less important challenges]."*

## 3. Connecting to a product vision statement

If a vision statement already exists, use it as a catalyst — read it and ask: *what does this
say that's distinct or foundational? What does it teach us about the product's value?* A
vision statement usually implies constraints worth naming explicitly (who the product is
*not* for, what it deliberately doesn't try to be, what's actually distinguishing vs.
generic).

If no vision statement exists, build one with Geoffrey Moore's template (from *Crossing the
Chasm*):

> *For [target customer] who [need or opportunity], the [product] is a [product category]
> that [key benefit]. Unlike [competitive alternative], our product [statement of primary
> differentiation].*

## 4. Key value exchanges

Even complex products usually have 3-6 essential moments where the customer's investment of
time/attention/energy/money is rewarded — visualize the customer journey and mark them. These
can happen *outside* the product itself (a concert-goer reaching their seat is a key value
exchange for a ticketing app) — don't exclude those. If possible, the North Star should
reflect one of these moments, not an internal proxy for it.

Examples: "the delivery arrives intact in under 30 minutes," "a collaborator makes their
first comment on your design," "your significant other says yes," "your medication arrives
before you need to call for a refill."

## 5. Mind-mapping inputs (worked bank example)

Once the North Star is set, generating inputs starts messy — that's expected. Technique:

1. Write the North Star (or a strong candidate) at the center.
2. Independently or as a group, dump every related concept, fear, belief, and metric idea
   around it — no filtering yet.
3. Cluster related sticky notes into named groups.
4. Distill each cluster into one high-level input, named and defined in a sentence.
5. Only then — last, not first — attach an exact measurable metric to each input.

Worked example (fictional financial institution, North Star = "We will be their primary
bank"):

Messy first pass: *primary bank?, we're integrated, their budgets, paying and spending,
recurring deposits?, acquisition at/below mkt rates, customers act, see insights and act,
borrowing, giving, acquisition, savings, direct deposit (first one), fund from lots of
sources, fast funding!, trust?, trustworthy?, we are reputable, timely and accurate,
integrated into financial life, clear the trust hurdle.*

Clustered into 5 inputs: **Funding effectiveness** (speed/variety of sources), **Understanding
and acting on insights** (insights drive spending/saving behavior), **Financial life
integration** (integrating the bank into different life areas), **Trust, reliability, and
reputation** (trust ≥ other major banks), **Conscious consumer acquisition** (at/below market
cost, the "right" high-LTV consumer).

Metrics attached last: funding effectiveness → *median time to <$500 funded*; understanding
and acting on insights → *# of users, last 7 days, with >82 spending health score*; financial
life integration → *# of users with ≥2 usage areas (spending/savings/investment), last 30
days*; trust/reliability/reputation → qualitative for now (research-dependent); conscious
consumer acquisition → *average CAC, last 30 days, under target*.

## 6. Testing inputs

Inputs that are too broad or lagging make it hard to focus effort ("satisfied customers");
inputs too narrow/prescriptive make it hard to find innovative solutions ("positive reviews on
social media"). Two tests:

- **Greenfield test.** Ignore the current roadmap. Ask: *"How many opportunities can you come
  up with in two minutes to influence this input?"* Format: *"We have an opportunity to
  improve [input] if we could [change in behavior/outcome]. Potential interventions include
  [features, experiments, etc.]."* Running out of ideas fast → the input is too narrow;
  drowning in scattered ideas → it's too broad.
- **Roadmap check.** Look at current work in progress and ask how each item connects to a
  chosen input. A clear link is good. An implicit link surfaces assumptions worth discussing.
  No link at all is a signal either the input set is missing a factor, or the work isn't
  actually valuable.

## 7. The North Star cycle of doubt

Normal pattern while converging: *"Yes, that metric idea makes sense"* → *"But how would we
measure that, exactly?"* → *"Oh wait, I think what we really want is something else
entirely"* → back to the top. This isn't failure, it's the process working. "Be willing to
just try on a North Star for size," John Cutler advises — pick one that's halfway decent,
work on inputs, then circle back and iterate. A powerful North Star needs to withstand
introspection; the first pass is often vague, and pressure-testing it is what sharpens it —
not a sign something went wrong.

## 8. When and how to change an established North Star

Signal to revisit: if the North Star stops directionally tracking the outcomes it's supposed
to predict (e.g. it's flat but the outcomes it should predict are moving, or vice versa), it's
time to revise. Real example: Amplitude changed its North Star from "Weekly Learning Users"
(individual-analyst focused) to a metric centered on **collaboration** when their strategy
shifted from serving individual analysts to growing team-wide usage and moving upmarket into
enterprise — the strategy shift came first, the metric change followed it, not the other way
around.

Signals the framework is working (worth naming to the user as progress, not just abstractly):
team members can trace their day-to-day work to the North Star; people say "no" to work more
easily and with evidence; shared vocabulary ("inputs," "our North Star") shows up even outside
the core team; confidence in decisions goes up.

Don't get too attached — the hardest part of changing a North Star is usually emotional
investment in the old one, not lack of evidence that it needs to change.

## 9. Levels of bets (connecting North Star to roadmap work)

"Bet" (not "experiment") is deliberately the vocabulary — it reads as safe-for-uncertainty and
doesn't trigger the "when will this team stop experimenting and get to work" reaction that
"experiment" sometimes does with stakeholders unfamiliar with product research.

| Level | Time horizon | Relationship | Burger King example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Years | Connects North Star to company/product success | "If we increase digital transactions, annual sales increase." |
| 1 | 1-3 quarters | Connects an input to the North Star | "If we improve new-user registration rate, total digital transactions increase." |
| 2 | 1-3 months | An opportunity to influence an input | "If we make mobile sign-up appealing, digital registrations increase." |
| 3 | 1-3 weeks | A concrete, sprint-sized intervention | "Add mobile-only discount coupons to make registration more appealing." |

Burger King's actual North Star was "digital transactions per user," with inputs new user
activation, registration, and frequency — a squad prioritized "mobile order coupons" to move
the frequency input specifically, tracing all the way up to the North Star.

Another real example: banking app **Dave** discovered in a North Star workshop that retention
was higher for users who added more recurring expenses to their account during onboarding.
They reworked onboarding to increase average expenses added — users who add expenses during
onboarding are 5.7x more likely to still be using Dave three months later.

## 10. OKRs, roadmaps, and prioritization

- **OKRs**: don't necessarily replace them — frame OKRs as the expected impact of Level 2 bets
  on one or more inputs. Watch for a team packing the quarter with deliverables instead of
  focusing on an outcome; that's the North Star losing its grip on the OKR process.
- **Roadmaps**: works best with theme-based roadmaps that account for uncertainty, not
  feature-and-date roadmaps. Prioritize opportunities most likely to drive an input, and
  follow up afterward on whether completed items actually had the expected impact — don't just
  mark "done" and move on.
- **Prioritization**: prefer a series of small bets over one big bet; prioritize by expected
  value/influence, not by how much work gets completed; watch out for big batches of work with
  no intermediate opportunity to learn and pivot.

## 11. Worked examples (for color / pattern-matching)

- **Netflix (2005, DVD era)**: North Star = "% of customers placing 3+ DVDs in their queue
  during their first session" — encapsulated the differentiation strategy; correlated with
  retention and, ultimately, subscription revenue.
- **Burger King**: "Digital transactions per user," inputs = new user activation,
  registration, frequency (see levels-of-bets table above).
- **Delivery app**: rejected "People Opening the App" / "Scheduled Deliveries" / "Early
  Deliveries" (all vanity-adjacent) in favor of "Happy Deliveries" (deliveries with zero
  issues) after research showed that's what actually drove retention and CLTV.
- **Fictional bank**: "We will be their primary bank" (see mind-mapping example above).
- **Dave**: recurring expenses added during onboarding as the retention driver (see levels of
  bets above).
- **Amplitude**: "Weekly Learning Users" (2+ people consuming a shared learning within 7
  days) → later changed to a collaboration-centered metric when the company's strategy shifted
  toward team-wide usage.
